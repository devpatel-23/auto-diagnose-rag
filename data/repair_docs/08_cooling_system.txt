COOLING SYSTEM COMPLETE GUIDE
==============================

SECTION 1: HOW THE COOLING SYSTEM WORKS
-----------------------------------------
The cooling system keeps the engine at its optimal operating temperature
(typically 195-220°F / 90-104°C). Too cold = poor efficiency and increased wear.
Too hot = engine damage or destruction.

Main components:
- Radiator: Dissipates heat from coolant to the air
- Water pump: Circulates coolant through the system
- Thermostat: Regulates when coolant flows to radiator
- Coolant reservoir/overflow tank: Handles expansion/contraction
- Radiator hoses (upper and lower): Route coolant between engine and radiator
- Heater core: Small radiator inside the dash that heats the cabin
- Cooling fan: Electric or mechanical, pulls air through radiator
- Head gaskets: Seal combustion chambers from coolant passages

Coolant flow path:
Engine block/head → Upper hose → Radiator (top to bottom) → Lower hose → Water pump → Engine

SECTION 2: COOLANT TYPES AND MAINTENANCE
------------------------------------------
Coolant Types:
IAT (Inorganic Additive Technology) - Green:
- Traditional green antifreeze
- Requires replacement every 2 years/30,000 miles
- Contains silicates and phosphates for corrosion protection
- Used in older vehicles (pre-1990s mostly)

OAT (Organic Acid Technology) - Orange/Red/Gold:
- Dex-Cool (GM), Toyota Red, Honda Blue (different colors, same technology)
- Lasts 5 years/150,000 miles
- Do NOT mix with IAT — creates a brown sludge that clogs passages
- Most vehicles 2000+ use some form of OAT

HOAT (Hybrid OAT) - Yellow/Gold:
- Combines both technologies
- Common in European vehicles (Mercedes, BMW, VW/Audi)
- 5 year service life
- Examples: Mercedes MB 325.0, BMW Blue (Pentosin), VW/Audi G13

P-OAT (Phosphate OAT) - Blue/Pink:
- Honda/Acura Blue, Toyota Super Long Life (pink/red)
- 5+ year life
- Phosphate protects aluminum — critical for Honda engines

CRITICAL RULE: Never mix different coolant types. If unknown, flush completely and start fresh with correct type.

Coolant Concentration:
- Standard: 50/50 antifreeze/distilled water
- Maximum protection: 70/30 antifreeze/water (below -84°F / -64°C)
- Never run straight antifreeze — actually has worse heat transfer than 50/50 and raises freeze point
- Always use DISTILLED water, never tap water (minerals cause scale buildup)

Testing Coolant Condition:
- Test strips: Check freeze protection, pH, nitrate/nitrite levels
- Refractometer: Accurate freeze point measurement
- pH should be 7.5-11.0 (acidic coolant corrodes aluminum quickly)
- Replace if pH below 7 or depleted additive levels

Coolant Flush Procedure:
1. Engine COLD — never open system when hot.
2. Place drain pan (coolant is toxic to animals — clean up completely).
3. Open radiator drain petcock or disconnect lower hose.
4. Flush with distilled water until clear.
5. For heavy contamination: Use flush additive, run engine briefly, drain again.
6. Refill with correct coolant/distilled water mix.
7. Bleed air from system (see air bleeding procedure below).

Air Bleeding Procedure (critical — air pockets cause overheating):
Methods vary by vehicle:
- Fill with thermostat open: Squeeze upper hose repeatedly while filling
- Open bleed screw: Some vehicles have a bleeder on the thermostat housing or top hose
- Idle with cap off: Warm up with overflow cap removed, let bubbles escape, top up
- Some vehicles require scan tool to command electric coolant pump for proper bleeding
Signs of air lock: Temperature gauge rises quickly then drops, heater blows cold, gurgling sounds

SECTION 3: WATER PUMP
-----------------------
Water Pump Function: Driven by timing belt/chain or serpentine belt. Circulates coolant under pressure.

Failure Modes:
1. Bearing failure:
   - Symptoms: Grinding/whining noise from front of engine (with timing belt water pump = internal)
   - Test: Grab pump shaft/pulley with engine OFF — any play = bad bearing
   - Coolant may leak from weep hole (small hole below shaft)

2. Impeller erosion:
   - Plastic impellers on some vehicles (Honda especially) can spin freely on shaft or erode
   - Symptom: Engine overheats despite correct coolant level (pump not moving coolant)
   - Diagnosis: Hard to detect without disassembly — if car overheats with new thermostat, suspect pump
   - Honda 1.5T (L15B7) known for plastic impeller issues

3. Seal/gasket failure:
   - Coolant leak from pump housing/gasket
   - Usually develop slowly — watch for puddles under engine

Water Pump Replacement Timing:
- Timing belt engines: ALWAYS replace water pump with timing belt
  (Labor is the same — pump is cheap insurance)
- Timing chain/serpentine belt engines: Replace when symptoms appear or ~100,000 miles as preventive

SECTION 4: THERMOSTAT
-----------------------
Thermostat Operation:
- Wax pellet expands at set temperature, opening valve
- Opening temperature stamped on thermostat (commonly 195°F / 90°C)
- Fully open by ~212-220°F (100-104°C)

Thermostat Failure:
Stuck CLOSED (overheating):
- Coolant cannot flow to radiator
- Rapid overheating, especially under load
- Diagnosis: Cold start — upper radiator hose should stay cold/cool for first few minutes,
  then get hot when thermostat opens. If hose gets hot immediately = stuck open.
  If hose never gets hot and engine overheats = stuck closed.

Stuck OPEN (cold running):
- Engine never reaches proper operating temperature
- Code P0128 (coolant temp below thermostat regulating temp)
- Poor fuel economy (ECU stays in warm-up enrichment)
- Heater may blow warm but not hot
- Engine takes very long to warm up in cold weather

Thermostat Replacement:
- Drain partial coolant (below thermostat level)
- Remove housing (usually 2-3 bolts)
- Note orientation of old thermostat before removal
- Clean mating surfaces of old gasket material
- Install new thermostat with jiggle pin (bleed hole) at 12 o'clock position unless specified otherwise
- New OEM-spec gasket/o-ring — do not reuse old gasket
- Refill coolant and bleed system

SECTION 5: RADIATOR
---------------------
Radiator Inspection:
- Physical damage: Bent fins reduce airflow (straighten with fine comb)
- External leaks: Inspect seams, fittings, plastic tank corners
- Internal blockage: Check temperature difference top to bottom with infrared thermometer
  (should be gradual drop — hot spots or cold spots indicate blockage)
- Electrolysis damage: Stray electrical current destroys aluminum radiators
  Check: Insert voltmeter probe into coolant, ground other lead to chassis. Over 0.4V = electrolysis.
  Fix: Check all ground straps, especially engine-to-chassis and engine-to-battery.

Radiator Flush vs Replacement:
- Chemical flush: Clears light scale and deposits. Use correct flush product.
- Reverse flush: Connect hose to bottom hose fitting, flow opposite to normal direction.
- Replacement: When leaking from core, tanks cracked, or severely corroded.

Radiator Hoses:
Inspection: Squeeze when cold — should be firm but pliable. Replace if:
- Hard and brittle (heat-hardened)
- Soft and mushy (starting to collapse internally — very dangerous)
- Cracked, abraded, or swollen at end fittings
- Any external cracks or bubbling

Replacement interval: Every 4-5 years or at major cooling system service.

SECTION 6: ELECTRIC COOLING FAN
----------------------------------
Electric Fan Operation:
- Controlled by ECU based on coolant temp sensor and A/C pressure
- Should come on at approximately 195-210°F (90-99°C)
- A/C turns on both fans in most vehicles

Testing Electric Fan:
1. A/C test: Turn on A/C — fan(s) should engage immediately
2. Bypass test: Apply 12V directly to fan connector — should spin freely
3. Relay test: Swap fan relay with identical relay from another circuit
4. Temperature test: With scan tool, watch coolant temp — fan should engage at its activation temp

Fan failures cause:
- Overheating only in traffic/at idle (not highway — ram air replaces fan at speed)
- A/C warm at idle even if cold at highway speed

Mechanical Fan Clutch (rear-wheel drive, older vehicles):
- Symptoms of failure: Overheating at idle, loud roaring from engine bay at cold start
- Test cold: Grab stationary fan blades (ENGINE OFF) — should turn with some resistance
  Spins freely = clutch failed
  Very hard to turn = seized clutch (creates loud roaring)

SECTION 7: HEAD GASKET FAILURE
---------------------------------
Head Gasket Warning Signs (in order of severity):
1. White smoke from exhaust (especially on warm start) — coolant burning in combustion
2. Coolant loss without visible external leak
3. Milky/foamy oil on dipstick or under oil cap (coolant in oil — very serious)
4. Bubbles in coolant reservoir (combustion gases entering cooling system)
5. Overheating with no other cooling system fault
6. Sweet smell from exhaust

Combustion Leak Test:
- Chemical test (Block Check): Add blue test fluid to coolant overflow while engine idling.
  If combustion gases present, fluid turns yellow-green. Very reliable test.
- Bubbles in overflow: Watch coolant overflow tank while revving engine — rising bubbles = combustion gas leak

Head Gasket Repair:
External sealer (temporary):
- Products like Bar's Leaks, K-Seal can temporarily seal minor leaks
- Not a permanent fix — buys time for proper repair
- Will not fix severe gasket failure

Proper repair:
- Head removal and inspection
- Machine shop: Resurface (mill) head if warped
- Measure head thickness — minimum spec stamped on head
- Inspect for cracks (pressure test, dye penetrant)
- New head gasket (OEM or MLS — Multi Layer Steel)
- Torque head bolts to spec in sequence
- Replace head bolts if torque-to-yield (TTY) type — do not reuse

Engines known for head gasket issues:
- Subaru EJ series (2.5L): Notorious for head gasket failures, especially on naturally aspirated
- GM 2.2L Ecotec: Timing chain, coolant bypass issues
- Ford 6.4L Power Stroke diesel: Dual head gaskets fail
- Chrysler 2.7L V6: Sludge-related failures
- Toyota 3.0L 3VZE (older 4Runner/T100): Head gasket issues, coolant in oil
